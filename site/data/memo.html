Memo: can telemetry tell us where to send the trucks?

To: Services Operations leadership
From: Arnav Chawla
Date: 2026-10-17
Repository: services-ops-intelligence, tag v0.2

The question

Every day a field organisation decides where to send a limited number of technicians. Some of those visits replace a part that was about to fail; some roll for nothing. This memo answers four questions on one quarter of data:

  1. If we can afford B truck rolls a day, how many of the drives that will fail in the next 30 days can a model put on that list, and how does that compare with the rule of thumb the industry already uses?
  2. Can last week's parts usage be forecast one week ahead better than "same as last week"?
  3. How often does a truck go back to the same asset, and how much of that is avoidable?
  4. Which cases could have been closed from a runbook without a person?

The data

The hardware telemetry is real: Backblaze publishes a daily health snapshot (SMART counters and a failure flag) for every hard drive in its data centres. The 2026 second quarter holds 31,968,003 drive-days, 359,101 drives, 81 drive models and 1,522 failures over 91 days (2026-04-01 to 2026-06-30).

Everything around the telemetry is generated: the 335 customer accounts, 701 sites, 8,281 support cases, 4,108 dispatches, 22 runbooks and 30 parts come from a seeded program in this repository that places every real drive at a customer site and opens cases around the real failure dates. The data is deliberately messy in documented ways, and the program keeps the ground truth, which is what makes the dispatch and deflection answers checkable.

What the model does

For each drive and each day t, the model gives the probability that the drive fails within the next 30 days, using only what was known on day t: the five SMART counters Backblaze found predictive, power-on hours, temperature, interface errors, bytes moved, drive model and capacity, and how the counters moved over the previous seven days. Three rankings were compared: Backblaze's rule (any of the five counters above zero), a logistic regression, and a gradient-boosted tree model (LightGBM) calibrated on drives it never trained on. The test is strict about time: training prediction dates 2026-04-08 to 04-24 (labels to 05-24), test dates 05-25 to 05-31 (labels to 06-30), so no test failure was ever a training label and nothing was tuned on the test window. The test holds 2,469,958 drive-days and 404 distinct failing drives.

The dispatch-budget result

On an average test day about 303 of the 353,000 drives are within 30 days of failing. Backblaze's rule flags 29,930 drives a day; if every flagged drive got a truck, 67% of the failures would be caught, at 0.7% precision. No operation can roll 30,000 trucks, so the useful comparison is at a budget.

In sentences: with 50 trucks a day, the model puts about 17 soon-to-fail drives on the list and 33 trucks roll for nothing (one visit in three finds a drive that fails within the month); the rule of thumb finds 2 or 3. With 100 trucks, the model finds 28 against the rule's 8. The logistic regression sits between the two.

The honest reading of the same table: even the best ranking catches under 10% of the coming month's failures with 100 trucks a day, because most of the 303 show no warning in their counters. The model orders the queue well; it does not make failures predictable. Precision-recall area: 0.099 for LightGBM, 0.057 for the logistic regression, 0.018 for the rule (random ordering 0.0009); half of all failures sit in the model's top 1% of drives. The calibrated probabilities are usable but rough (top decile predicts 0.81%, observes 0.61%), and with 404 failing drives in the test these numbers move by a few points between reasonable windows. Read them as "several times better than the rule at any budget", not as "16.9".

Parts demand

Weekly parts usage per region and part was forecast one week ahead over the last six weeks of the quarter (13 weeks exist, so a monthly backtest was not possible). Average absolute error per region-and-part series, where the average week uses 1.2 units: last-week naive 0.84, four-week average 0.79, exponential smoothing 0.73, pooled LightGBM 0.68. Summed to fleet totals per part, exponential smoothing was best (2.00 against 2.37 for naive) and the LightGBM under-forecast as demand fell through June. A simple smoother beats "same as last week" by about 15%; the learned model adds little. The demand is generated: the pipeline and the comparison are proven, the demand is not.

Repeat dispatches

A repeat is a second or third visit on the same case, or a first visit within 30 days of a previous dispatched case closing on the same asset. Of 4,108 dispatches, 1,138 (27.7%) are repeats: 1,001 on the same case and 137 across cases. Against the ground truth, 472 were necessary (follow-ups after a missing part or an unfinished visit on a real fault) and 666 avoidable. Drive-failure cases repeat often (29%) but rarely avoidably (11.5%); configuration, how-to and firmware cases repeat 23% to 30% of the time with every repeat avoidable, because there was no hardware fault. Forty percent of first visits were unnecessary by the same truth. These rates reproduce the generator's rules; they describe the method, not a real operation.

Which cases could a runbook have closed?

A case is a deflection candidate when its description reads closely enough like one of the 22 runbooks. Two text matchers were run, TF-IDF and BM25 (the scoring behind classical search); each case was scored against the runbooks for its product family and flagged when its best match cleared a threshold. The threshold was chosen on one half of the 8,281 cases and the result measured on the other half, against the generator's record of which cases were in fact deflectable (2,252, or 27%).

On the held-out half TF-IDF flags 1,604 cases, 806 of them deflectable: precision 50%, recall 70%. BM25: 41% and 68%. One policy rule, that only severity 3 and 4 cases can be deflected, lifts TF-IDF to 69% precision at 67% recall. On how-to, configuration and firmware cases 70% to 74% of flags are right and 69% to 83% of deflectable cases are found; with every runbook eligible, the matcher's top runbook is the one the generator used for 98% of deflectable cases. Where it fails is the useful part: drive-failure, performance and connectivity cases match a runbook just as closely, because a runbook exists for the symptom, and almost none are deflectable, because the fault is real.

The caveat matters more here than anywhere else in this memo. The generator decided deflectability first (from category, severity and a coin) and only then copied a runbook phrase into the case text; a non-deflectable case in the same category carries a stock phrase that is also a runbook's first symptom. So the text can tell a matcher that a runbook covers the symptom, which is necessary for deflection, and nothing about the severity gate or the coin. The 50% and 70% measure how close a matcher gets to that ceiling on templated text, not what it would do on real case text. The number to trust is the runbook hit rate: the matching itself works. The family filter costs recall (97% to 70%) because the generator assigns a case to another family's runbook when its own family has none, a property of a 22-entry library, not of the method.

What is real and what is generated

Real: the telemetry, the failure labels, the failure model, every number in the dispatch-budget table, the ranking metrics, and the drive-level risk list. Generated: which customer owns which drive, the cases and their text, the runbooks, the dispatches, the parts used, and therefore every number in the parts, repeat-dispatch and deflection sections. The generated numbers test that the code answers the question correctly (the ground truth is known); they do not describe any customer.

What would change with a year of data

  • Months of test days and thousands of failures instead of 404: error bars on the budget table, calibration per drive model.
  • Monthly and seasonal parts backtests, and a forecast tied to the failure model (expected failures by site next month, hence sleds to stage).
  • Repeat rates by technician and supplier would stop being noise (most technicians have fewer than 60 visits this quarter).
  • Real case and dispatch records in place of the generator: "avoidable" and "deflectable" would need a human-reviewed sample, and the deflection matcher would be judged on real case text.
